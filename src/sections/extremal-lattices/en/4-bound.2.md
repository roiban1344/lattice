The combination was chosen to make the coefficients of $q$ and $q^2$ vanish. With three products we can impose three conditions: the constant term is $1$, and the coefficients of $q$ and $q^2$ are $0$. These conditions determine the combination, and with it the coefficient of $q^3$: it is $52\,416\,000$. So if an even unimodular lattice of dimension $48$ has no vectors of norm $2$ or $4$, its theta series is this combination, and it has $52\,416\,000$ vectors of norm $6$. Either way, every even unimodular lattice of dimension $48$ has a nonzero vector of norm at most $6$.

The same reasoning works in every dimension. Write $m=\lfloor n/24\rfloor$. There are $m+1$ products, so we can require the constant term to be $1$ and the coefficients of $q,\dots,q^m$ to vanish; these conditions determine the combination, and with it all coefficients. A little more thought shows that the minimum can never exceed $2m+2$: what is needed is that the coefficient of $q^{m+1}$ in the fixed combination is always positive, and Siegel proved that it is [[Sie69]](#ref-Sie69).

> **Theorem** (Mallows–Odlyzko–Sloane [[MOS75]](#ref-MOS75)). An even unimodular lattice of dimension $n$ satisfies
> $$
> \min\Lambda\le 2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{24}\right\rfloor+2 .
> $$

An even unimodular lattice is called **extremal** if equality holds. The bound rises by $2$ each time the dimension reaches a multiple of $24$: it is $2$ in dimensions $8$ and $16$, $4$ in dimensions $24$ to $40$, $6$ in dimensions $48$ to $64$, and so on. $E_8$ and the Leech lattice are extremal.

The theta series of an extremal lattice is the fixed combination, so the number of vectors of each norm is known before the lattice itself is found. The second table lists the number of vectors of minimal norm. The coefficients of the fixed combination are not always positive, and since a number of vectors cannot be negative, a negative coefficient rules out an extremal lattice. Mallows, Odlyzko and Sloane showed that a negative coefficient appears once the dimension is large enough, and Jenkins and Rouse showed that this happens in every dimension greater than $163\,264$, so there are no extremal lattices in those dimensions [[JR11]](#ref-JR11). In dimensions up to $163\,264$, whether an extremal lattice exists has to be decided dimension by dimension. The [history](../history/) page lists the dimensions in which examples are known.
